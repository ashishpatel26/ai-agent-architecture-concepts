4 — Hierarchical Orchestration

Delegate through supervisory layers that preserve domain ownership, limit context, and return evidence-rich results to a single accountable decision maker.

Concept overview

Hierarchical orchestration organizes agents into supervisors and subordinate workers. A top-level supervisor chooses domains and integrates their results; each domain supervisor decomposes work within a narrower capability boundary. The hierarchy is useful when different teams own different tools, policies, and evaluation criteria.

Every delegation adds latency and a chance to lose constraints. Supervisors should pass explicit contracts rather than conversational instructions, and workers should return findings with provenance rather than polished summaries alone. Hierarchy should follow real decision ownership. Replicating an organization chart without independent responsibilities produces unnecessary layers.

Why it matters in production

Proof of concept

A prototype can use one supervisor and two workers to test whether bounded delegation improves quality or tool isolation.

Production

Production needs delegation identifiers, depth limits, budget inheritance, and cancellation propagation. A child cannot expand authority beyond the permissions granted by its parent contract.

Enterprise scale

At enterprise scale, domain supervisors become separately operated services. Version their contracts and track both local task success and whether their results help the parent complete its actual objective.

Architecture components

Root supervisor

Owns the overall objective, cross-domain priorities, and final acceptance.

Domain supervisors

Translate business subproblems into specialist assignments under inherited constraints.

Leaf workers

Perform narrow investigations using domain-specific tools and evidence formats.

Delegation registry

Records parent-child relationships, budgets, lease ownership, and cancellation state.

Evidence aggregator

Preserves source references and unresolved disagreements across supervisory layers.

The architecture, mapped

Runtime flow

  1. 1

    Frame objective

    Record the business decision, forbidden actions, and total cost and time budgets.

  2. 2

    Select domains

    Dispatch only to supervisors whose capabilities and permissions match the requested scope.

  3. 3

    Subdivide locally

    Domain supervisors create bounded child tasks and reserve budget for aggregation.

  4. 4

    Execute leaves

    Workers retrieve evidence or calculate results without receiving unrelated organizational data.

  5. 5

    Aggregate upwards

    Each layer validates completeness and reports limitations alongside findings.

  6. 6

    Accept or escalate

    The root resolves cross-domain conflict and obtains approval before consequential execution.

Assessing a regional port closure

Supply chain · Illustrative enterprise scenario, not a reported customer outcome.

Business situation

An electronics manufacturer must understand how a port closure affects component availability, shipping options, and working capital.

The problem

A flat collection of workers generates incompatible recommendations because transport and finance evaluate different assumptions.

Agent requirement

The assistant must produce feasible alternatives with explicit cost, timing, and inventory assumptions.

Architecture

Supply and finance supervisors coordinate their specialists under a root disruption supervisor using one scenario identifier.

Runtime

  • The supply branch establishes feasible arrival dates and inventory gaps, then publishes its assumptions as versioned artifacts.
  • The finance branch evaluates those artifacts; the root compares options and routes a recommendation to operations leadership.

Failure scenario

If finance analysis is unavailable, the system publishes a logistics-only assessment marked unsuitable for spending approval.

Operational impact

Measure hierarchy depth, repeated evidence retrieval, assumption mismatch, and total decision latency before adding more supervisory levels.

Architecture tradeoffs

DecisionBenefitCost
Domain autonomyTeams can evolve specialist methods behind stable contracts.Cross-domain integration requires disciplined version management.
Narrow contextWorkers receive less irrelevant or sensitive information.Important global constraints can disappear during delegation.
Layered validationDomain errors can be caught near their source.Multiple synthesis stages add latency and may distort nuance.

Failure scenarios & recovery

Supervisor recursively delegates its own task

Detect: Registry detects repeated task fingerprints or excessive depth.

Recover: Stop recursion and return a bounded unresolved result.

A child outlives the parent deadline

Detect: Lease monitor finds running descendants of a terminal task.

Recover: Propagate cancellation and reconcile any already-started side effects.

Common architecture mistakes

Giving every layer the full budget

Allocate child ceilings from one shared parent allowance.

Returning summaries without assumptions

Include evidence, input versions, and uncertainty in every result contract.

Adding supervisors to match departments

Introduce a layer only when it owns a distinct coordination decision.

When to choose this pattern

Choose it when

  • Domain teams own separate tools and acceptance criteria.
  • Complex requests need local decomposition and cross-domain synthesis.
  • Sensitive context should remain within domain boundaries.

Avoid it when

  • All workers operate on the same small evidence set.
  • Interactive latency cannot tolerate multiple delegation hops.
  • The hierarchy lacks explicit authority and budget inheritance.

Architecture checklist

  • Limit maximum supervisory depth.
  • Carry constraints through every delegation contract.
  • Reserve budget for final aggregation.
  • Propagate cancellation to all descendants.
  • Preserve assumptions with returned evidence.
  • Evaluate parent success rather than leaf completion alone.
