Tenant-scoped incident and change assistant

Combine tenant symptoms, release history, and bounded diagnostics into engineer-approved incident recovery.

Explore in the architecture studio

The business situation

Illustrative reference architecture. Validate requirements for your organization.

Illustrative architecture: A software provider sees intermittent failures for a subset of enterprise tenants after a release. Support and engineering need to connect customer reports, telemetry, feature flags, and recent changes without exposing one tenant's data to another. Agents investigate and propose actions while on-call engineers retain production authority.

Problem

Shared infrastructure makes correlation difficult: a noisy tenant can dominate aggregate metrics, and sensitive payloads may leak through logs. A guessed rollback or unrestricted diagnostic query can worsen the incident or cross tenant boundaries.

Why a multi-agent system?

Tenant-context, telemetry, change-analysis, and runbook specialists operate with explicit scopes. A deterministic incident coordinator sets diagnostic budgets, correlates release versions, and requires approval for consequential remediation.

Intended outcome

The intended result is a reviewable operational decision with source evidence and explicit ownership. Evaluate investigation quality and containment as well as restoration time. The workflow should reduce repetitive evidence gathering while keeping production authority, customer boundaries, and uncertain causal explanations explicit.

Architecture diagrams

Runtime flow

  1. 1

    Register incident scope

    Bind support cases and telemetry signals to an incident, affected tenants, service versions, and on-call owner.

  2. 2

    Collect bounded evidence

    Run approved queries with row limits and redaction, preserving query versions and source freshness.

  3. 3

    Compare cohorts

    Separate affected and unaffected tenants by release, configuration, and infrastructure placement without asserting causality from correlation alone.

  4. 4

    Propose discriminating checks

    Select diagnostics that distinguish hypotheses and fit the incident's time, cost, and resource budget.

  5. 5

    Review recovery action

    Present exact targets, expected effects, rollback prerequisites, and unresolved evidence for on-call approval.

  6. 6

    Execute and verify

    Apply the approved action through the gateway, compare service indicators against declared checks, and stop on unexpected cohort expansion.

Failure recovery

A diagnostic query omits its tenant predicate.

Detect: Gateway policy rejects the query before execution.

Recover: Return a structured scope error and require a corrected query; do not fall back to a broad shared search.

A feature-flag rollback affects more tenants than approved.

Detect: Target-set comparison detects a larger evaluated cohort.

Recover: Block the mutation and ask the engineer to review the exact expanded scope.

Security & decision boundaries

  • Derive tenant scope from authenticated incident context, never model-supplied free text alone.
  • Redact credentials and customer payloads before observability data reaches agents.
  • Bound diagnostic queries by time range, tenant set, rows, and execution budget.
  • Require on-call approval for deployments, flags, schema changes, and customer communications.
  • Verify post-action indicators and halt on unexpected blast-radius expansion.

Operational metrics

Measurement definitions, not promised improvements.

Incident evidence latency

Time from incident declaration to a scoped, source-backed hypothesis packet.

Diagnostic discrimination

Executed checks that eliminate or confirm a hypothesis divided by executed checks.

Tenant boundary violations

Unauthorized cross-tenant data returns, measured separately from correctly blocked attempts.

Recovery verification time

Elapsed time from approved action receipt to completed declared service checks or escalation.
